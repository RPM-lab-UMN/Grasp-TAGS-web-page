Grasp-TAGS

Task-Aware Grasp Selection via Critic Distillation
for Prehensile Loco-Manipulation

Xun Tu, Minghao Zou, Karthik Desingh
University of Minnesota, Twin Cities
Paper

Abstract

Loco-manipulation, in which mobile manipulators coordinate locomotion and manipulation over long horizons, is an important paradigm for large-object rearrangement in service robotics and warehouse automation. Reliably executing these tasks remains challenging because performance depends on complex robot-object interaction dynamics. Existing learning-based approaches reduce the need for accurate dynamics models, but typically assume a fixed or predefined initial grasp. In practice, different feasible grasps can lead to substantially different downstream manipulation performance.

We propose Grasp-TAGS, a task-aware grasp selection framework for goal-conditioned large-object rearrangement. A unified PPO policy is trained across multiple initial grasp configurations, and its learned critic is distilled into a lightweight grasp utility network that ranks candidates using only pre-interaction grasp and goal information. At inference time, the highest-utility grasp is selected before the downstream policy executes. Simulation experiments on household objects show improved rearrangement success over fixed single-grasp policies and random grasp selection, together with lower final orientation error across the evaluated objects.

Task-Aware Grasp Selection

Large objects such as chairs, carts, and buckets require coordinated base and arm motion to reach a desired planar pose. Although several candidate grasps may be feasible and stable, each establishes a different robot-object interaction configuration. Those configurations affect controllability, long-horizon behavior, and ultimately task success.

Grasp-TAGS treats the initial grasp as a task decision rather than a fixed setup step. For a rearrangement goal, it evaluates the expected utility of every candidate grasp and selects the one most suitable for the downstream prehensile manipulation task.

Method

The framework uses a hierarchical controller: a pretrained low-level locomotion controller maintains stable base motion, while a high-level PPO policy produces task-level base-velocity and arm-joint commands. During training, each episode samples one feasible initial grasp and a target object pose. Training a single grasp-conditioned policy across candidates captures the interaction dynamics of multiple grasp configurations while yielding a common value function for comparison.

The PPO critic estimates long-horizon return from the initial state and therefore encodes the expected downstream utility of each grasp. Because the critic depends on simulator-only physical information, Grasp-TAGS distills its predictions into a grasp utility network that receives only the candidate grasp configuration and goal information available before contact. The distilled network ranks the candidates at inference time, and the selected grasp initializes the learned manipulation policy.

  • A unified goal- and grasp-conditioned loco-manipulation policy trained across multiple feasible grasps.
  • A critic-distilled utility network for selecting a grasp before physical interaction.
  • Simulation evaluation on chairs, carts, and buckets with goal-conditioned planar rearrangement tasks.

Results

We evaluate in IsaacLab on eight household objects: three chairs, two carts, and three buckets. The comparison includes the best policy trained for an individual grasp, a unified grasp-conditioned policy with random candidate selection, and the full Grasp-TAGS pipeline. Across all evaluated objects, task-aware selection achieves the highest success rate and consistently reduces final angular error.

For Chair1, Grasp-TAGS reaches a 93.65% success rate, compared with 75.88% for random grasp selection and 69.02–78.20% for the individual single-grasp policies.
